How the predictor will work

The predictor turns 26 years of match results into three numbers for every upcoming Premier League game: the chance of a home win, a draw and an away win. This page goes through each step in the order the code runs, so you can check that each choice makes sense.

01The big picture

The system has three layers. Only the last one learns from data. The first two are fixed rules that turn the match history into numbers the model can use.

LayerWhat it isLearns from data?
Elo ratingsA formula that updates each team's strength rating after every gameNo. Its 4 settings are tuned once.
FeaturesAbout 30 numbers per match that describe both teams before kickoff. Elo is one of them.No
Logistic regressionTurns a match's features into three probabilitiesYes. Training finds one weight per feature for each outcome.

02The data

  • Results come from football-data.co.uk: 9,980 Premier League and 14,532 Championship matches since August 2000, with scores, shots and betting odds. Real data
  • The schedule for this season comes from openfootball: dates, kickoff times and matchweeks for all 380 games.
  • Team names differ between the two sources ("Manchester United FC" against "Man United"). A table maps 56 openfootball spellings to 37 football-data names. It was built by matching scored games between the sources.

Each row in the results table describes a match after it ended. The model can't use those numbers to predict the same match, because nobody knows the score before kickoff. The next three sections turn the history into numbers that were known before each kickoff.

03Walking through time

The code goes through all matches in date order, one date at a time. For each date it does three steps, always in this order:

  1. Read each team's current state: rating, recent games, last match date.
  2. Record one feature row for each game on that date.
  3. Apply that date's results: update the ratings and add the games to each team's recent list.

Why this matters. Data leakage means a feature contains information from after kickoff. A model trained on leaked data looks excellent in testing and fails on real games, because real games never give it that information.

Why a whole date at a time. Home advantage uses the league's recent results (section 4). If the 15:00 results were applied before the 17:30 game's row was recorded, that row would contain information from the same afternoon.

How we test it. Change the result of the last match in the data, rebuild the table, and check that every earlier row is exactly the same.

04Elo ratings

Elo gives each team one number for its strength. Every team starts at 1500 in August 2000, and the formula updates both teams after each game. An upset moves the ratings a lot. An expected result moves them a little. We calculate the ratings ourselves from the results; they aren't downloaded.

Step 1: the expected score

Before a match, the rating gap gives an expected result for the home team:

\[ E_{\text{home}} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-\left(R_{\text{home}} \,+\, H \,-\, R_{\text{away}}\right)/400}} \]
\(E_{\text{home}}\)
expected score for the home team, between 0 and 1
\(R_{\text{home}}, R_{\text{away}}\)
the two ratings before the match
\(H\)
home advantage in rating points (step 4)
\(400\)
sets the scale: a 400-point gap gives 0.91, and a gap of 0 gives 0.5
The expected score counts a win as 1 and a draw as 0.5. It is not the chance of winning. Logistic regression (section 6) turns the ratings into chances of a win, a draw and a loss.

Step 2: the win-margin multiplier

Bigger wins move ratings more, but each extra goal adds less:

\[ G = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{draw or 1-goal win} \\[2pt] 1.5 & \text{2-goal win} \\[2pt] \dfrac{11 + N}{8} & \text{win by } N \ge 3 \text{ goals} \end{cases} \]

These values come from the World Football Elo ratings. A 3–0 counts 1.75 and a 7–0 counts 2.25, so one thrashing doesn't dominate a team's rating.

Step 3: the update

\[ \Delta = K \cdot G \cdot \left(S - E_{\text{home}}\right) \qquad R_{\text{home}} \leftarrow R_{\text{home}} + \Delta \qquad R_{\text{away}} \leftarrow R_{\text{away}} - \Delta \]
\(S\)
the actual score: 1 for a home win, 0.5 for a draw, 0 for an away win
\(K\)
how fast ratings move (tuned in step 7)
\(\Delta\)
the rating change. The home team gains exactly what the away team loses.

Step 4: home advantage that changes over time

Before each match, the code measures how well home teams did over the league's last few hundred games, and turns that into rating points:

\[ p = \text{average home score over the league's last } N_{\text{home}} \text{ games} \qquad H = 400 \, \log_{10} \frac{p}{1 - p} \]

This is the expected-score formula run backwards: \(H\) is the boost that makes two equal teams produce the home score we actually saw. For example, \(p = 0.58\) gives \(H \approx 56\) points, and \(p = 0.5\) gives 0.

Step 5: summer shrink

Every August, each rating moves part of the way back to its league's average:

\[ R \leftarrow \bar{R} + (1 - s)\,\left(R - \bar{R}\right) \]

\(\bar{R}\) is the league average and \(s\), between 0 and 1, is the shrink. With \(s = 0.2\) and an average of 1500, a team at 1900 starts the next season at 1820, and a team at 1350 starts at 1380. Example Squads change over the summer, so last season's gaps are partly gone.

Step 6: promoted and relegated teams

Premier League and Championship teams never play each other in our data. So a Championship 1650 and a Premier League 1650 are on different scales, the way 1,650 yen isn't 1,650 dollars. A promoted team's starting rating is converted:

\[ R^{\text{PL}}_{\text{start}} = \bar{R}^{\text{PL}}_{\text{relegated}} + a \left( R^{\text{Ch}} - \bar{R}^{\text{Ch}} \right) \]
\(\bar{R}^{\text{PL}}_{\text{relegated}}\)
average end-of-season rating of the teams relegated that summer
\(R^{\text{Ch}} - \bar{R}^{\text{Ch}}\)
how far above the Championship average the promoted team was
\(a\)
how much of that carries over, between 0 and 1 (tuned in step 7)

Step 7: choosing the four settings

SettingMeaningValues tried
\(K\)How fast ratings move10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40
\(s\)Summer shrink toward the average0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.33, 0.5
\(N_{\text{home}}\)Games used to measure home advantage190, 380, 760
\(a\)How much Championship strength carries over0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1

The code tries all 450 combinations (a grid search) and keeps the one whose expected scores were closest to the real results, measured by the mean squared error:

\[ \text{MSE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( S_i - E_i \right)^2 \]

For example, if \(E = 0.82\) and the home team wins, the error is \((1 - 0.82)^2 = 0.03\). If it loses, the error is \((0 - 0.82)^2 = 0.67\). Lower is better.

05The features

Each feature row describes both teams before kickoff. Every per-team feature appears twice, once for the home team and once for the away team, for example home_ppg_5 and away_ppg_5. Most form features use a rolling window: an average over the team's last few games before this match.

\[ \text{ppg}_5 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{points}_j \qquad n = \min(5,\ \text{games played so far}) \]
\[ \text{vs\_exp}_5 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left( S_j - E_j \right) \qquad \text{rest\_days} = \min\left(21,\ d - d_{\text{prev}}\right) \]

Here \(S_j\) and \(E_j\) are the actual and expected score in each recent game, and \(d - d_{\text{prev}}\) is the number of days since the team's previous league game.

Rules for every feature

  • Only the past. Every number for a match on date D comes from matches before D (section 3).
  • Missing values stay empty. For example, the first weeks of 2000-01, or the one Championship match with no shot data. Milestone 3 decides how to fill them.
  • One code path. The same code builds rows for past matches and for upcoming games. A test checks that both give the same row for a past match.
  • Both leagues get rows. Championship rows have a league column. Milestone 3 tests whether training on them helps Premier League predictions.

06The model: logistic regression

Logistic regression takes the ~30 features of a match and returns three probabilities that add up to 100%: home win, draw, away win. It's the only part of the system that learns from data. Training finds one weight per feature for each outcome, using about 9,000 past Premier League matches.

Why not linear regression?

Linear regression predicts a number along a straight line. Give it a big enough rating gap and the straight line predicts more than 100% or less than 0%, which is impossible for a probability. Logistic regression bends the line into an S-curve that always stays between 0 and 1. It also handles the three outcomes directly. Linear regression would need home, draw and away turned into a made-up number scale.

How it turns features into probabilities

Each outcome gets a score \(z\): a starting value \(b\) plus each feature \(x\) times its weight \(w\). Then softmax turns the three scores into probabilities:

\[ z_k = b_k + w_{k,1}\,x_1 + w_{k,2}\,x_2 + \dots + w_{k,n}\,x_n \qquad k \in \{\text{home},\ \text{draw},\ \text{away}\} \]
\[ P(k) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{e^{z_{\text{home}}} + e^{z_{\text{draw}}} + e^{z_{\text{away}}}} \]

\(e^{z}\) is always positive, and dividing by the total makes the three add up to 1. In practice one outcome's score can be fixed at 0 as the reference. That gives the same probabilities with fewer weights, so below the draw score is 0. The features are first rescaled to an average of 0 and a spread of 1, so their weights can be compared.

The draw score is fixed at 0, yet the draw probability is highest when the match is roughly even and falls as the gap grows. That comes from softmax: when one side's score rises, everything else gets a smaller share.

How it learns: log loss

Training adjusts the weights step by step until the average log loss over the training matches is as low as it can get:

\[ \text{log loss} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln p_i \]

\(p_i\) is the probability the model gave to what actually happened in match \(i\). A confident wrong prediction costs far more than a cautious one, so the model learns to be honest about uncertainty.

Try it: the cost of one prediction

60% costs 0.51, 25% costs 1.39, and 5% costs 3.00. For comparison, giving every outcome 33.3% costs 1.10 every time.

Keeping the weights sensible: regularization

\[ \text{minimize} \quad \text{log loss} + \lambda \sum w^2 \]

\(\lambda\) (lambda) sets a penalty for large weights. Without it, the model can fit quirks of past seasons that won't happen again. That's called overfitting. Milestone 3 picks \(\lambda\) by testing on seasons the model didn't train on.

Why logistic regression

  • Real probabilities. Its output is exactly what the site shows.
  • Readable weights. You can say which features moved a prediction. That's what the "key factors" on match cards will use later.
  • Enough data. It works well with about 9,000 training rows. More complex models usually need more data to beat it.
  • An honest baseline. It's a standard first model in football prediction, so it sets a fair bar for fancier models.
  • Fast. It trains in seconds, so the daily update can retrain every day.

Its limit: each feature's effect on the score \(z\) is a straight line. It can't discover combinations by itself, such as "strong team and tired", unless we build that combination as a feature. That's why a later milestone tries gradient boosting, which can find combinations, using the same evaluation.

The draw problem

About a quarter of games end level, but a draw is rarely the single most likely outcome. In the 2,660 games of the test seasons, the bookmakers never once made a draw the favourite. Their highest draw probability was 32.3%. Real data Our model will behave the same way. So the site shows all three probabilities, and says "Close call" when the top two are within 5 points, instead of always naming a pick.

07Judging the model

Milestone 3 scores the model on seasons it never trained on, and compares it with three benchmarks. These are their real scores on the test seasons, 2019-20 to 2025-26 (2,660 matches): Real data

BenchmarkAccuracyLog loss
Always pick the home team43.4%—
Predict the average shares every game (46.6% / 25.1% / 28.4%, from 2002-03 to 2018-19)43.4%1.074
Bookmakers (average odds, margin removed)54.5%0.968

Accuracy is the share of games where the most likely outcome happened. Log loss (section 6) rewards honest probabilities, and lower is better. Log loss is the main score. The average-shares benchmark always rates "home" highest, so its accuracy equals always picking home.

The goal: clearly beat the first two, and get as close as we can to the bookmakers. They use injuries, lineups and the money of everyone betting, so matching them would be a strong result. Their probabilities come from the odds:

\[ q_k = \frac{1/\text{odds}_k}{1/\text{odds}_{\text{home}} + 1/\text{odds}_{\text{draw}} + 1/\text{odds}_{\text{away}}} \]

The three \(1/\text{odds}\) add up to more than 1 because of the bookmaker's margin, 4.5% on average in these seasons. Dividing by the total removes it.

Walk-forward testing

For each test season, the model trains on every season before it and is scored on that season. This copies real use: the model always predicts a season it hasn't seen.

Calibration is the last check. When the model says 60%, those games should go that way about 60% of the time. Milestone 3 plots this.

08Checks before the model

These four checks run at the end of milestone 2. They confirm the Elo code works, so a bug can't hide inside the model in milestone 3. They don't measure how good the predictions are; section 7 does that.

  1. End-of-season Elo against the final table. For each finished season, rank teams by their Elo in May and compare that with the real table. A surprise team doesn't break this: a team that starts low and keeps winning gains points all season, so by May its rating includes that season. Leicester in 2015-16 is the classic case. Passes if the rank correlation averages at least 0.8. The same comparison with August ratings is shown for information only, because that one is a forecast.
  2. Elo alone against the average shares. A logistic regression with only \(E\) as input, trained on 2002-03 to 2014-15 and scored on 2015-16 to 2018-19, must have a lower log loss than predicting the average shares every game.
  3. Ratings chart. Ratings since 2000 for the clubs with the most Premier League seasons. Sudden unexplained jumps or flat lines show bugs that numbers can hide.
  4. Promoted teams. Promoted teams that went straight back down should have started with lower ratings, on average, than the ones that stayed up.
